# Evidence Summary: After-Hours Work and Recovery

Research on work extending into evenings and weekends generally finds that the effect on
wellbeing depends less on the absolute number of after-hours minutes and more on whether it is
occasional (project-driven, self-initiated, time-bound) or sustained (recurring most days, driven
by workload backlog rather than choice). Sustained after-hours work erodes psychological
detachment from work, which is one of the stronger predictors of next-day fatigue and reduced
next-day performance.

This is why a single evening of catch-up work is not treated as a signal, but 5+ consecutive
working days with meaningfully elevated after-hours activity is: it suggests the person has
stopped being able to fully disconnect, independent of whether the underlying cause is workload,
habit, or personal choice. A "shutdown reminder" - a simple nudge to define an end-of-day
boundary - is a low-friction response that respects employee autonomy while surfacing the pattern.
