# Evidence Summary: Meeting Load and Cognitive Fatigue

Back-to-back meetings and frequent context switching between different types of work both draw
on the same limited pool of attentional resources. Studies of knowledge work consistently
associate a high rate of context switching with lower reported focus quality and higher
end-of-day fatigue, independent of total hours worked. The mechanism is less about the total
volume of work and more about fragmentation: many short, high-switching-cost work windows are
more depleting than fewer, longer, uninterrupted ones.

Practically, this means two people who work the same total hours can experience very different
strain depending on how fragmented their day is. A rising context-switch rate alongside falling
"focus minutes" (time in uninterrupted work sessions) is a specific, actionable pattern - the
appropriate response is protecting blocks of calendar time, not necessarily reducing total
workload.
