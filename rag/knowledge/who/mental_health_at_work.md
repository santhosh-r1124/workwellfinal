# Mental Health at Work: Core Principles

Workplace mental health guidance from public health bodies generally converges on a few
recurring principles. First, psychosocial risks - excessive workload, low control over one's
own schedule, poor role clarity, and lack of support - are treated as organizational hazards to
be managed, not individual weaknesses to be fixed. Second, prevention works best when it acts on
working conditions before problems become severe: reasonable workloads, predictable hours, and
manager training to recognize early signs of strain are more effective than crisis response
alone. Third, early, low-friction support (a schedule adjustment, a workload conversation, a
short break) is preferable to waiting until an employee is in visible distress.

For an early-warning system like WorkWell, this translates into a specific stance: detect
sustained deviations from a person's own normal pattern, explain them in plain language, and
offer the least intrusive helpful action first. A single unusual day is normal variation, not a
signal. A sustained change across multiple work-pattern signals, especially one that persists
after accounting for plausible workplace context (a release, a reorg, a seasonal deadline), is
the kind of evidence worth surfacing to the employee themselves - not a basis for a diagnosis,
and not something to report to a manager as an individual score.
